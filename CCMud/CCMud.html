Persistent World Design Reference

A generated world provides the geography. Player actions create its history. Time transforms what they leave behind.

Nick's Notes · The Big Idea

This is not a traditional MUD made from thousands of numbered rooms. It is a persistent coordinate world that becomes detailed where people actually live, explore, build, fight, and leave their mark.

1. One Continuous Coordinate World

The outdoor world is based on coordinates rather than numbered rooms. A character may stand at a precise world position, and physical things occupy positions and dimensions around that character.

Coordinate Standard

World positions use signed 64-bit integer coordinates, with one coordinate unit equal to one inch. This provides exact inch-level position granularity while leaving a coordinate range vastly larger than the playable world could ever require. PostgreSQL can store these values directly as BIGINT; Python integers comfortably represent them as well.

1 unit = 1 inch 12 units = 1 foot 1,200 units = 100 feet 63,360 units = 1 mile Signed 64-bit range: -9,223,372,036,854,775,808 through +9,223,372,036,854,775,807

At one-inch granularity, the positive half of a signed 64-bit coordinate alone extends roughly 145 trillion miles from the origin. The practical size of Crown & Call's world can therefore be chosen entirely for gameplay reasons rather than datatype limitations.

The World Origin

(0,0) is the mathematical center of the coordinate system. Coordinates are signed naturally around that origin rather than artificially offsetting the entire world into enormous positive numbers.

NORTH +Y ↑ | WEST | EAST -X ←────── (0,0) ─────→ +X | ↓ -Y SOUTH

The convention is simple: east is positive X, west is negative X, north is positive Y and south is negative Y. Elevation uses Z, with positive and negative values around an appropriate reference such as sea level.

X = east / west Y = north / south Z = elevation Example: Character at (+1200, +600) = 100 feet east and 50 feet north of the origin. Character at (-126720, -63360) = 2 miles west and 1 mile south of the origin.

The origin is an engine reference point and need not have special importance in the fiction, although locating the initial development area reasonably near it makes coordinates easier for developers to read and debug.

Distance and Heading

Negative coordinates do not complicate spatial mathematics. Distance, direction, perception and movement normally depend on the difference between two positions. Even an artificially all-positive coordinate system immediately produces negative displacement values whenever a target lies west or south of the observer.

Observer: ( 5321, -48999) Target: ( -125, 4999) dx = -125 - 5321 = -5,446 inches dy = 4999 - (-48999) = 53,998 inches The target is therefore mostly north and slightly west of the observer.

Headings can be derived from the displacement with ordinary Cartesian mathematics such as atan2. Integer coordinates guarantee exact one-inch positions; calculated distances and headings may naturally be fractional values. For example, points one inch apart on both X and Y are approximately 1.414 inches apart diagonally.

Signed coordinates also make world geography easier for developers to understand at a glance. A negative X and positive Y immediately identifies the northwest quadrant; positive X and negative Y identifies the southeast quadrant.

Player-Facing Movement

Coordinates are an engine concept, not a burden placed on the player. Players navigate naturally: north, follow road, approach rider, go tavern, go light. The engine translates intention into movement through coordinate space. Players ordinarily never need to see raw coordinate values.

Coordinate rule: Signed 64-bit X/Y/Z integers use one-inch units. The coordinate system is centered on (0,0), with +X east, -X west, +Y north and -Y south.
Scale is not the enemy. A world a million square miles across does not require a million square miles of active simulation. Most of the world can remain mathematical geography until something happens there.

2. Procedurally Generated Wilderness

The untouched wilderness does not need to be instantiated tree by tree, rock by rock or blade of grass by blade of grass. Procedural generation describes the character and resources of the landscape: biome, elevation, moisture, terrain, vegetation density, dominant species and similar environmental properties.

LANDSCAPE AT (x,y) terrain: gently rolling grassland moisture: moderate tree cover: light dominant tree: oak ground cover: tall grass seed: 83719244

Given the same seed and coordinates, the generator produces the same underlying landscape whenever that area is needed. An oak-forested area does not require thousands of generated Oak objects. It simply provides oak as part of the local landscape.

Interaction Creates Specific History

If a character cuts down an oak in suitable oak woodland, the engine does not need to identify a pre-existing procedural tree at an exact coordinate. The landscape supplies the tree. The player's action creates the specific things that now matter: logs, a stump, work performed and any meaningful change to the surrounding land.

PROCEDURAL LANDSCAPE Light oak woodland ↓ Character cuts an oak ↓ PLAYER-CREATED HISTORY Oak logs + oak stump ↓ Time and environment act on the traces ↓ Traces eventually disappear ↓ Procedural baseline remains

Small actions need not rewrite the terrain itself. One stump in oak woodland does not mean the forest is now materially different. If repeated activity becomes significant—such as clearing a large area—the world can store a broader landscape modifier describing reduced tree cover, a clearing, field, burn scar or similar change.

Generation rule: Do not instantiate nature merely because it could be interacted with. Instantiate the persistent consequences when someone actually changes it.

3. Persistence by Exception

The generated landscape is the baseline. The database stores history and meaningful differences from that baseline.

PROCEDURAL WORLD Oak woodland ↓ Jake chops an oak ↓ PERSISTENT HISTORY Logs created Stump created at Jake's position cut_at = 2031-05-12 ↓ Stump weathers, becomes obscured and decays ↓ Stump eventually disappears ↓ No exception remains; procedural baseline is enough again

This is the heart of world persistence. An untouched acre may need almost no stored detail. An acre where people have lived for twenty years may contain a rich history of construction, clearing, trails, ruins and possessions. As temporary traces disappear and nature reclaims an area, obsolete exceptions can eventually be removed.

“The world becomes more detailed where its history happens.”

4. Player Construction & Homesteading

A player can travel deep into wilderness, clear land and create a place that never existed in the original world design. Cabins, wells, fences, fields, barns, roads, trails and other alterations become persistent world objects.

HOMESTEAD #127 Owner: Jake Location: (1,847,450, 3,291,080) Cabin Barn Well Fence Field Woodpile Firepit Wagon track Stumps Fruit trees

The homestead does not need to become a special numbered “Jake's Homestead Room.” It remains part of the same coordinate world. Another player can genuinely discover it by physically reaching that location.

Construction Is Physical

Construction materials must actually be brought to the worksite. A Craft cannot silently consume lumber, stone or tools merely because they are owned by the character or happen to exist hundreds of feet away in the same open Space. Materials carried by the character or staged within the defined work area are eligible; distant materials are not.

Plain-language rule: If you are going to use it, get it to where you are going to use it.

This makes hauling, wagons, roads, storage, workshops and the location of natural resources meaningful without inventing separate logistics mechanics. A stack of lumber beside an unfinished cabin is a real collection of world objects that another character can perceive and, subject to ownership rules, interact with.

5. Chunks & Spatial Indexing

The world is divided internally into invisible chunks. Chunks are an engine optimization used for spatial lookup, generation, persistence and simulation; players never see them.

┌───────┬───────┬───────┐ │ C41 │ C42 │ C43 │ ├───────┼───────┼───────┤ │ C51 │ JAKE │ C53 │ ├───────┼───────┼───────┤ │ C61 │ C62 │ C63 │ └───────┴───────┴───────┘

Spatial indexes prevent perception from becoming “search every object in the world.” Nearby queries examine only nearby cells/chunks. Long-distance queries examine only entities capable of mattering at long distance.

Chunk size and indexing strategy are implementation details to tune experimentally; they should never become gameplay concepts.

6. Sleeping & Waking Areas

Most of the world is dormant. A cabin, fence, stump and abandoned field can all continue to exist without consuming active CPU time.

DORMANT Persistent state exists; no active simulation ↓ player approaches WAKING Load local changes + calculate elapsed effects ↓ ACTIVE Movement, NPCs, combat, sound, light, interaction ↓ nobody remains nearby SLEEP Persist meaningful changes + timestamp state

Waking is driven by interaction range, not by mere visibility. Seeing a mountain thirty miles away should not wake the mountain's local simulation.

Foundational rule: Wake what is close enough to interact with. Query what is far enough to perceive.

7. Time, Decay & Wilderness Reclamation

Time does not require continuous simulation. If nobody visits Jake's abandoned homestead for five years, the server does not need to simulate weeds growing hour by hour.

last_updated = 2031-05-01 area_wakes = 2036-09-01 elapsed = 5 years, 4 months apply_elapsed_change(area, elapsed)

When the area wakes, long-term systems calculate the accumulated effects of elapsed time. Reclamation can depend on biome, climate, materials, construction quality and previous state.

Elapsed TimePossible Changes
MonthsFields weed over, trails soften, wood weathers.
Several yearsBrush overtakes fields, fence sections collapse, stumps rot, saplings appear, roofs deteriorate.
DecadesYoung forest reclaims fields, roads nearly disappear, cabins become ruins, wells collapse, old fruit trees may remain as evidence.

The Stump as a Model of History

A freshly cut oak becomes a persistent stump. Years later, when the area wakes, it may become weathered and moss-covered. Decades later it may rot away entirely while nearby trees reclaim the opening. No daily “stump update” is required; its current state is derived when someone next has reason to care.

Living Oak → Fresh Stump → Weathered Stump → Rotting Stump → Gone ↑ player-created history

Randomness may influence these transformations, but results should be stable and deterministic enough that repeatedly loading an area does not make its history change arbitrarily.

8. Spaces, Buildings & Boundaries

Coordinates answer where something is. A Space describes the environmental and perception context that contains it. Spaces can range from a jail cell to a hundred-square-mile plain.

WORLD └── Rolling Plains └── Town of Westhaven ├── Market Street ├── Market Square ├── Crown & Stag Tavern │ ├── Common Room │ ├── Kitchen │ └── Upstairs └── Blacksmith

A 50 × 100 foot tavern has real boundaries within town coordinates. While a character is inside, walls constrain perception to the interior. Stepping through its front door may move the character only a few feet but changes the active perception space from Tavern to Street.

Buildings Are Geometry

A constructed building literally occupies its dimensions in the game world. A 15 × 15 foot cabin encloses those 225 square feet at the coordinates where it was built. Entering the cabin does not teleport the character to an unrelated numbered room; the character crosses the doorway into the bounded interior while remaining at the corresponding world coordinates.

15 × 15 CABIN NORTH ↑ ┌───────────────┐ │ │ │ INTERIOR │ │ 15 × 15 │ │ │ └───────┬───────┘ │ door

Walls constrain movement because walls physically occupy the boundary, not because a room lacks a north exit. Interior objects have positions of their own. A chest can sit against the west wall, a bed in the northeast corner and a lantern near the center. Larger structures can contain nested Spaces such as offices, kitchens, stalls or storerooms.

Player construction therefore changes navigable geography. Buildings can block movement and sight, shelter occupants from weather, muffle sound and contain portals such as doors and windows. The exact sophistication of collision and line-of-sight calculations can grow over time without changing this underlying model.

“Buildings are not destinations. Buildings are geometry.”

Portals

Doors, windows, gates, cave mouths and stairways connect spaces. Their state can control movement, vision, sound and light. A closed tavern door might block movement and vision while muffling sound; opening it allows those effects to cross the boundary.

Vertical Levels

Multiple floors do not require traditional room IDs or a special floor system. A level is defined by its elevation, ceiling height, floor type and ceiling type. Characters may occupy the same X/Y position on different Z elevations while remaining in separate Spaces. Floors and ceilings normally separate sight and sound; stairs simply provide the legitimate route for changing elevation and entering the adjoining level.

Basement elevation -96" First floor elevation 0" Second floor elevation +120" Same X/Y, different Z and containing Space.

Hearing between levels can remain deliberately simple. The normal radius-based hearing check still identifies nearby listeners; Space and boundary information may then suppress or reduce what crosses a floor, wall, grate or other opening. A solid floor may convey only footsteps or indistinct murmurs while an open grate can allow clearer speech. Exact acoustics are not a priority and should not become a simulation burden.

9. Generated Perception Instead of Static Room Descriptions

look does not merely print a stored room paragraph. It asks what the character can perceive from the current position and Space, then generates prose from the answer.

LOOK ↓ Where is Jake? ↓ What Space contains him? ↓ What nearby and distant things are perceptible? ↓ How much detail can he perceive? ↓ Generate the description

An outdoor result might mention the muddy road underfoot, a sign nearby, a town wall behind, smoke miles away and a mountain on the horizon. Each observation corresponds to something that actually exists.

Perception Bands

BandIllustrative ScaleExamples
Immediate0–15 ftMug, sword, coin, nearby person

Distant1,000 ft–2 miTown, tower, smoke, large fire

These are conceptual bands, not fixed final ranges. Crucially, a ten-mile query does not inspect every coin, sword, stump and squirrel within ten miles. It searches only indexes containing entities capable of being perceived at that scale.

One Object, Several Perceptions

LANTERN physical object visible nearby light source visible much farther at night illumination affects a small surrounding area CAMPFIRE fire local visibility light long night visibility smoke long daylight visibility sound shorter range smell separate range

At close range a player may see “a brass lantern beside the road.” Far away at night the same entity may produce only “a faint yellow light flickers to the northeast.” In daylight, nothing may be noticeable from that distance.

Distance Changes Detail

The same person might be “a figure on the road” at long distance, “a man carrying a shield” closer in, and finally “Jake carrying a battered shield” at recognition distance. Perception is not merely visible/not-visible; it controls how much information is available.

Existence Does Not Guarantee Perception

A persistent object may physically exist for years without being obvious to a passing character. Objects and traces have perceptibility appropriate to their size, age and condition. A freshly cut stump may be noticeable at useful distance; months later grass and brush may hide it except at close range even though the stump itself may take years to rot away.

Illustrative stump lifecycle Fresh stump → relatively conspicuous nearby Months old → weathered; grass and brush reduce noticeability Older stump → easy to miss except at close range Snow-covered → may be effectively invisible Years old → decayed and increasingly obscure Eventually → gone

Weather and environment modify perception without necessarily modifying the persistent object. Snow can conceal a low stump; tall summer grass can obscure tracks or debris; darkness, fog and brush can reduce what is noticed. When those conditions change, the underlying object need not be recreated because it never ceased to exist.

Movement, Attention & Search

Perception depends on what the observer is doing. A character galloping across country has far less opportunity to notice subtle traces than one walking, standing still or deliberately searching. Perception therefore should not be implemented as a simple rule that reveals every object inside a fixed radius.

Observer StateGeneral Awareness
Galloping / very fast travelVery poor for subtle nearby details
Jogging / hurried movementReduced
WalkingNormal passive perception
Standing stillImproved opportunity to notice details
Active search / trackingHigh attention to subtle evidence

The same hidden stump forty feet away might go unnoticed while walking past it, become noticeable after stopping, and be very likely to be found during a deliberate search. Exact distances and modifiers are balance values rather than fixed rules at this stage.

> north You continue across gently rolling grassland. Scattered oaks stand along the ridge to the west. > stop You stand among knee-high grass beneath scattered oaks. An old stump is partly concealed by brush nearby. > search You study the surrounding ground carefully. Several weathered oak stumps are hidden among the grass, and faint depressions suggest wagons once passed this way.

This creates a meaningful tradeoff between travel speed and awareness. A traveler may cover ground quickly and miss subtle evidence, or travel cautiously and notice more. Deliberate searching and tracking can reveal historical traces that ordinary passive perception would omit.

Perception rule: Reveal what a character has a reasonable opportunity to notice, not everything technically within geometric range.
“History can physically exist without being immediately discoverable.”

Speed and Perception Are Inversely Related

Travel speed and detailed perception should be treated as opposing demands on the character's attention. The faster a character moves, the less opportunity there is to notice subtle evidence. Slowing down allows more attention to the immediate environment. At the extreme, tracking or deliberately casting around for sign is not ordinary travel with a bonus; it is a very slow form of physical searching.

MORE SPEED ←────────────────────────────→ MORE PERCEPTION Gallop Ride Jog Walk Cautious Search / Track Cover more ground Examine more ground Notice less detail Notice more detail

Exact speeds and perception values remain subject to playtesting, but the relationship itself is foundational. A character galloping across country may perceive only obvious terrain and major features. A walking character receives normal passive perception. A character deliberately searching or tracking may move extremely slowly while closely examining only a narrow area around the path.

Tracking Is Physical Searching

When tracking, the character casts around for sign: disturbed grass, prints, broken vegetation, wagon ruts, displaced stones, cut wood and other physical evidence. The effective detailed-search area may be only approximately 10–15 feet around the character. This is an initial design range rather than a locked balance value.

Direction of intended travel ────────────────────────────────────→ /\ /\/\ /\ _________/ \______/ \_____/ \____ casting for sign Detailed search reaches only a short distance around the character's actual path.

Tracking therefore does not function as radar. If the relevant tracks lie forty feet from the character's search path, they may simply be missed. When a trail disappears, finding it again may require physically casting outward from the last known sign and spending real world-travel time covering the surrounding ground.

This also creates a meaningful travel decision. Moving quickly covers more territory but sacrifices information. Moving cautiously or tracking covers far less territory but greatly improves the opportunity to discover subtle traces. The two effects are intentionally inverse rather than independent bonuses.

Tracking rule: Tracking increases detailed perception by sacrificing movement speed. A character searches the ground actually covered; the command does not reveal tracks across an arbitrary surrounding radius.

10. Natural Movement & Spatial Communication

Generated perceptions retain references to the entities that produced them. If the player sees “a faint light to the east,” the command go light can resolve that phrase to the actual light source. The same idea supports go mountain, approach tavern, follow rider and go smoke.

Speech also uses position instead of “send to everyone in the room.” A whisper may carry only a few feet, normal speech farther, and a shout much farther. Walls and closed doors can reduce or block sound.

whisper very short range say conversational range speak loudly larger local range shout long local range Exact distances are gameplay values to tune.

This permits two people at opposite ends of a large hall to experience communication differently from two people sitting together at the same table.

11. Genuine Discovery & Emergent History

A remote homestead can genuinely remain undiscovered. The game need not advertise “Jake's Cabin — 150 miles northeast.” Another player may instead discover an old wagon track, follow it through the woods, notice chimney smoke, and eventually find the clearing.

Years after abandonment, that same player might find only a sagging cabin, collapsed fence, overgrown field and old fruit trees. Nobody needed to author an “Ancient Abandoned Farm” quest. It became old because someone built it, lived there, left it, and time passed.

“The generated world provides geography. Player actions create history. Time transforms that history.”

This is the intended character of the world: players do not merely consume locations created by designers. They can create locations that become part of the world's archaeology for later players.

12. CAPs, Crafts & Learning

Crown & Call should avoid a traditional skill system. CAPs describe what a character is capable of; Crafts describe practical knowledge the character has actually learned. Characters develop by choosing new Crafts whose prerequisites and CAP requirements they satisfy.

Core rule: A learned Craft never fails randomly. If the character knows the Craft and meets its requirements for tools, materials, conditions and time, the Craft succeeds. Crafting never requires a die roll merely to determine whether gathered materials are wasted.

CAP Progression & Boost Tokens

CAP advancement is separate from Craft learning. Crafts may require minimum values in one or more CAPs, but learning or performing a Craft does not itself raise those CAPs. Characters develop their eight CAPs through CAP Boost Tokens.

Characters begin with 15 total CAP points distributed among the eight CAPs. Each individual CAP ranges from 0 through 20. Normal character progression ends at a provisional total CAP of 100, allowing 85 CAP increases beyond the starting character.

Earning CAP Boost Tokens

CAP Boost Tokens are awarded on a fairly regular basis through a combination of real-world time and meaningful play. Merely allowing time to pass is not enough for full progression, and simply remaining connected to the game is not meaningful activity. The system should consider patterns such as frequency of active play, active session duration and a reasonable level of actual interaction.

Command activity may help distinguish genuine play from an idle connection—for example, an average on the order of twenty meaningful commands per active hour could be one useful signal—but raw command count must never become advancement currency. Repeated filler commands, AFK automation or similar behavior should not accelerate development.

The exact token cadence and activity calculation remain balance values to be determined through playtesting. The intended result is that a regularly active character earns tokens every few days, while an infrequently played character may still progress at a reduced rate rather than receiving nothing.

Progression principle: Real time permits character growth, while meaningful play demonstrates activity. Neither idle account age nor sheer hours connected should dominate CAP progression.

Buying CAP Increases

CAP Boost Tokens may be banked and spent whenever the player chooses. The cost of increasing a CAP is equal to the new CAP level. The earning requirements for tokens do not become progressively harder; instead, advancement slows naturally because higher CAP values cost more tokens.

The Natural CAP Ceiling

Total CAP 100 is the ceiling for automatic progression, not necessarily the absolute highest total a character can ever attain. When an increase brings a character's total CAP to 100, the character's remaining banked CAP Boost Tokens are immediately reduced to zero and automatic CAP Boost Token earning permanently ends for that character.

Character reaches TOTAL CAP 100 Remaining CAP Boost Tokens → 0 Automatic token earning → permanently ends Individual CAP maximum remains 20.

The interface must warn the player before confirming an increase that will reach total CAP 100, including the number of banked tokens that will be forfeited. Players therefore know the consequence before making the final normal-progression purchase.

Administrator Roleplay Awards

Administrators may award a small number of CAP Boost Tokens—typically 1 to 3—for exceptional roleplay or similarly outstanding contribution to the shared game world. These are discretionary recognition awards rather than a repeatable activity system and should not be tied to a mechanical quota such as hours roleplayed.

Administrator awards use the same CAP Boost Tokens as ordinary progression. They may be awarded even after a character reaches total CAP 100. Because automatic token earning has permanently ceased at that point, administrator-awarded tokens provide the only means by which an exceptional character can eventually raise total CAP above 100. The normal escalating purchase cost still applies, and no individual CAP may exceed 20.

Discretionary awards should be logged with the administrator, character, amount, date and reason so that exceptional advancement remains accountable and auditable.

NORMAL DEVELOPMENT Real time + meaningful activity ↓ CAP Boost Tokens ↓ Purchase CAP increases ↓ TOTAL CAP 100 ↓ Banked tokens become 0 Automatic token earning ends ↓ ADMIN RECOGNITION ONLY Exceptional roleplay → occasional tokens ↓ Possible advancement beyond total CAP 100
CAP rule: Tokens are earned at a relatively steady rate; higher CAPs cost progressively more. Normal progression ends at total CAP 100, while exceptional roleplay recognized by administrators can permit rare advancement beyond that natural ceiling.

Knowledge Progression

Advanced Crafts are reached through prerequisite Crafts rather than being immediately available. Knowing how to fell a tree does not imply knowing how to mill lumber, and knowing how to mill lumber does not imply knowing how to frame a cabin. A character deliberately chooses what to learn next; new Crafts are not unlocked randomly.

CAP REQUIREMENTS + PREREQUISITE CRAFTS ↓ CHOOSE AN AVAILABLE CRAFT ↓ REAL-WORLD LEARNING TIME ↓ CRAFT LEARNED ↓ NEW CRAFTS MAY BECOME AVAILABLE

Prerequisites should form a knowledge graph rather than a single rigid tree. One Craft may support several later disciplines, and an advanced Craft may require knowledge drawn from several branches.

Fell Tree ──→ Prepare Log ──→ Hew Timber ──┐ │ Basic Joinery ────────────────────────────────┤ ↓ Timber Framing │ ┌───────────────────┴──────────────┐ ↓ ↓ Wall Framing Roof Framing └───────────────┬──────────────────┘ ↓ Cabin Construction

CAPs Gate Knowledge; They Do Not Create Failure Rolls

A Craft may require one CAP or a combination of CAPs before it can be learned. Higher or different CAP requirements can distinguish simple practical knowledge from advanced work without creating separate numerical skills. Difficulty is expressed through prerequisites, CAP requirements, tools, materials and work time—not an arbitrary percentage chance to fail.

CRAFT: Timber Framing Prerequisite Crafts: Fell Tree Hew Timber Basic Joinery CAP Requirements: ATHLX 3 ACADX 4 Learning Time: 7 real-world days

General Crafts, Specific World States

Crafts should remain as broad and reusable as practical knowledge permits. Construction need not have a separate Craft for every tiny stage or building variation. A general Build Foundation Craft, for example, can produce foundation objects that later building-specific Crafts consume or transform.

Build Foundation ↓ Foundation(s) │ ├── Frame Cabin ──────→ Cabin Frame ──────→ Finish Cabin ├── Frame House ──────→ House Frame ──────→ Finish House └── Frame Commercial ─→ Commercial Frame ─→ Finish Commercial

Scale can come from quantities and world state rather than multiplying nearly identical Crafts. A cabin might require one suitable foundation while a commercial building might require four. Exact quantities, dimensions and construction stages remain design values to be worked out.

The world object carries the specific state. A foundation knows its dimensions and location; a framed cabin knows that it is a cabin under construction. Crafts define meaningful transformations of those objects.

Proximity Resolves Craft Targets

Crafting should respect the coordinate world. When several valid targets exist, the nearest valid target within the Craft's interaction range is normally used. A held or carried valid object takes precedence over nearby ground objects. The player therefore resolves most ambiguity by physically positioning the character rather than by attaching object numbers to every Craft command.

Three deer carcasses nearby: > butcher deer Uses the nearest valid carcass. Want a different one? > approach 2.deer > butcher deer Carrying a carcass? > butcher deer The held carcass takes precedence.

The same rule applies to construction. A character approaches the intended cabin, worksite, forge, tree or other target and then performs the Craft. Ordinal targeting can remain useful for commands such as approach 2.cabin, but commands such as build cabin or butcher deer should not normally require numbered-object syntax.

Required materials follow the same physical principle. Carried materials and materials within the Craft's defined work area may be consumed; materials hundreds of feet away do not count merely because they are visible or owned by the character.

Deterministic Crafting

Once a Craft is learned, performing it is deterministic when its requirements are met. Better CAPs or better tools may eventually influence sensible factors such as work time or efficiency, but they should not turn a known Craft into a gambling mechanic.

KNOWLEDGE + CAPABILITY + TOOLS + MATERIALS + TIME ↓ SUCCESS

Atomic Craft Execution

Crafts are deliberately blunt and transactional. There are no timed Craft steps, delayed room echoes or intermediate Craft states. When a Craft command is issued, the server validates every requirement before changing anything. If any requirement fails, nothing is consumed and nothing is created. If all requirements pass, material consumption, product creation and Work Timer debt are committed together as one atomic operation.

VALIDATE EVERYTHING ↓ requirements fail → CHANGE NOTHING ↓ requirements pass ↓ CONSUME + CREATE + ADD WORK TIME ↓ COMMIT → “You craft a linen shirt.”

A disconnect or server failure must never leave a character with consumed materials but no finished product, or a finished product without its required materials having been consumed. Other characters cannot interfere halfway through a Craft because there is no mechanically exposed halfway point.

Roleplay around crafting remains entirely voluntary through EMOTE, communication and parenthetical roleplay on the Craft command. The Craft engine itself does not force players to wait through descriptive stages.

“Validate → Commit → Done.”

Keyed Crafts

A single Craft may use one key to select a variation from the material or consumable actually supplied. For example, Tailor Shirt can accept linen, cotton, wool, silk or another permitted cloth and produce the corresponding material shirt without requiring a separate Craft for every material. A later dye Craft may key from the dye color. Five materials and twelve colors can therefore yield sixty shirt variations without sixty shirt Crafts.

The input determines the keyed result. Raw bolts need not accept every transformation merely because a property exists; for example, dyeing may be valid for finished garments while raw linen bolts are not valid dye targets.

One Craft, one key. Let the input determine the variation.

Work Timer

Performing Crafts consumes abstract work capacity through a single character Work Timer. The Craft's result occurs immediately when the command succeeds; its Work Time is then added to the character's accumulated work debt. The timer continuously winds down in real-world elapsed time whether the character is online or offline.

A character may begin a Craft only while the current Work Timer is below 48 hours. The 48-hour value is a starting threshold, not a maximum. A Craft begun at 47 hours may add another 48 hours and leave approximately 95 hours of work debt. The character remains free to play normally but cannot begin another Craft until the timer falls below the threshold again.

Current Work Timer: 47 hours Craft Work Time: 48 hours ↓ Craft succeeds immediately New Work Timer: 95 hours ↓ Real-world time passes online or offline ↓ Below 48 hours → Crafting available again

Small Crafts use the same mechanism. Gathering or preparing something might add only minutes, while a major construction operation may add many hours. There is no need for separate major/minor Craft timer systems.

An implementation can store the debt as a future timestamp rather than decrementing a counter. When work is performed, its duration is added to the later of the current time or the existing work-until timestamp. This naturally handles offline recovery without periodic timer ticks.

Work Timer is labor debt, not a job-completion timer. The work happens now and the world changes now. The timer represents how much work capacity the character has already spent.

Learning Timers

Each Craft has a Learning Time. There is no separate learning cooldown. A character may learn only one Craft at a time, and the Learning Time itself controls the rate of knowledge advancement.

All Learning Times use real-world elapsed time. They continue while the player is offline. Learning must never require remaining logged in, accumulating in-game hours, performing repetitive practice actions, or pausing ordinary play.

learning_craft_id = TIMBER_FRAMING learning_completes = 2026-09-28 10:30 if current_time >= learning_completes: add craft to known crafts clear active learning character may immediately choose another craft

Basic Crafts may take hours or a day or two to learn. Advanced Crafts may require weeks. Exact durations are balance values to be determined through playtesting; long timers should represent genuinely substantial bodies of knowledge rather than making trivial techniques tedious.

Learning Is a Commitment

Once a character begins learning a Craft, that choice is committed until the Learning Time completes. Learning cannot be paused, swapped, banked or partially preserved. This deliberately avoids tracking fractional learning progress and prevents a secondary system of resumable learning queues.

Currently Learning: Timber Framing Completes: September 28, 2026 A new Craft cannot be started until Timber Framing has been learned.

Because the commitment may last days or weeks, the interface must clearly show prerequisites, CAP requirements, Learning Time and the exact completion date before the player confirms the choice.

The Learning Timer and Work Timer are independent. A character may continue learning one new Craft in the background while performing Crafts already known, subject to the Work Timer.

Avoid Craft Explosion

A Craft should represent a meaningful body of practical knowledge, not every tiny variation of an action. The system should not recreate a giant traditional skill list under a different name. For example, a general body of pastry knowledge may ultimately be more appropriate than separate advanced Crafts for every individual pie.

Open design question: Specific recipes, plans or patterns may eventually form a layer beneath broader Crafts—for example, a Pastry Craft with individual recipes, or a construction Craft with individual building plans. This distinction is promising but is not yet a locked rule.

Specialization Without Classes

Because learning consumes real-world time, characters can specialize organically without hard classes. One character may spend months developing forestry and construction knowledge while another develops mining, smelting and metalworking. Nothing necessarily forbids eventually learning both, but time makes broad mastery a meaningful long-term investment.

“CAPs measure ability. Crafts grant knowledge. Tools enable work. The world provides materials.”

Craft design remains under active development. Prerequisite graphs, CAP combinations, Learning Times and the possible recipe/plan layer should be expanded as the system is discussed and tested.

13. Combat & Engagements

Combat should use the Crown & Call rules directly rather than an automated MUD combat loop that rapidly exchanges attacks until somebody dies. Combat is turn-based, giving every participant time to make meaningful decisions: attack, defend, move, flee, use an item, make a Calling, Gather, or take another appropriate action.

Core direction: Combat is an open, spatial Engagement resolved in rounds. The world does not freeze around the combatants, and the original participant list is not protected by an invisible force field.

Rounds Represent World Time

A combat round currently represents approximately 7 seconds of world time. Players may be allowed substantially longer in real time to choose their actions—perhaps as much as a minute—but the time spent by humans deciding does not become extra time for their characters or for distant characters elsewhere in the world.

ROUND 1 world time 14:32:00–14:32:07 ROUND 2 world time 14:32:07–14:32:14 ROUND 3 world time 14:32:14–14:32:21 ... ROUND 9 approximately 63 seconds of world time The players may have spent roughly 9–10 real minutes making those decisions. The characters have not.

Actions should generally be declared during the round and then resolved by the server. This avoids a five-person battle taking five times as long merely because every combatant must wait through a traditional sequential turn order. Exact declaration windows and resolution details remain to be designed and playtested.

Engagements Are Open

An Engagement records the characters actively affecting a fight, but participation is not permanently locked when combat begins. If four characters fight in a crowded tavern, the people standing nearby can watch, flee, shout for help or choose to interfere. A bystander who attacks, grabs or otherwise meaningfully affects a combatant can enter the Engagement at an appropriate round boundary.

This also preserves forced combat. A character cannot refuse an attack merely because that character did not voluntarily join an encounter, and combatants cannot become untouchable to the surrounding world merely because their fight began first.

“Joining an Engagement means affecting the fight, not entering an instance.”

Ranged Participation

A character does not need to move into a melee cluster to participate. A nearby archer, for example, may enter an Engagement by firing at a combatant from the character's actual position. A magic user may likewise affect the Engagement from whatever range the relevant Calling permits.

Tom ↔ Sam Jake 80 ft away Jake: > shoot Tom Jake remains 80 feet away. If the shot is valid, Jake becomes a participant at the appropriate round boundary.

Range, line of sight, cover and geometry remain real. A ranged participant can retreat, advance, seek cover or be charged by another combatant. Combat never collapses all participants into an abstract room or common melee position.

Reinforcements Must Physically Arrive

A persistent multiplayer world creates a special problem when outside communication is available. A character being attacked may contact friends through Discord or another external service. The game cannot prevent that communication, but it can prevent distant characters from exploiting the slow real-time pace of turn-based combat.

When an Engagement begins, a future system can snapshot the physical positions of characters and determine the earliest combat round at which each could legitimately reach or affect the Engagement. Travel time is converted into combat rounds using the approximate seven seconds of world time represented by each round.

ENGAGEMENT BEGINS Gus participant Sam participant Rachel nearby → earliest intervention: Round 4 Jake 250 yd away → earliest intervention: Round 15 Max 2 mi away → much later Malfoy 5 mi away → much later still Illustrative only; actual values depend on movement rate, terrain, range and other conditions.

A nearby character may therefore be able to run toward the fight and join after several rounds. A character five miles away cannot convert ten real minutes spent while the combatants choose nine actions into ten minutes of character travel; only about one minute of combat-world time has elapsed.

The Causal Travel Envelope

The future reinforcement system should constrain not merely the final arrival round but how much progress a distant character could legitimately have made toward the Engagement. If Malfoy is five miles away and could not arrive until Round 100, then at Round 50 he can have covered at most approximately half of that legitimate journey toward the fight.

Malfoy starts 5 miles away Earliest arrival: Round 100 Round 0 → 5.00 miles away Round 25 → no closer than about 3.75 miles Round 50 → no closer than about 2.50 miles Round 75 → no closer than about 1.25 miles Round 100 → may legitimately arrive

The Engagement ending must not erase this constraint. Otherwise distant reinforcements could wait for the fight to finish, use compressed travel to arrive immediately, and attack the survivor. The event's causal travel envelope can continue expanding after combat ends until the restriction no longer matters.

This is deliberately preferable to temporary immunity or “safe passage.” Gus is not protected from attack after surviving a fight. Anyone who could physically have reached Gus may attack him. Someone five miles away simply remains five-miles-worth-of-time away.

This reinforcement/travel-envelope system is a future implementation, not a requirement for the first playable combat prototype. The initial architecture should preserve the information needed to add it later: authoritative positions, Engagement round counts and a fixed amount of represented world time per round.

World Time & Movement Scale

Crown & Call uses a 6× world-time multiplier. Six hours pass in the game world for every real-world hour, so one complete 24-hour game day lasts four real hours. This allows day, night, weather and other time-dependent conditions to change meaningfully during ordinary play.

WORLD TIME MULTIPLIER: 6× 10 real minutes = 1 game hour 1 real hour = 6 game hours 4 real hours = 1 full game day

Ordinary physical movement uses a separate, fixed 4× speed multiplier. A real-world human walking pace of approximately 4 mph therefore becomes a baseline game-world walking speed of approximately 16 miles per game hour. Because six game hours pass during one real hour, uninterrupted walking covers approximately 96 world miles per real hour.

BASE WALKING SPEED 4 mph × 4× movement multiplier = 16 world mph With 6× world time: 16 world mph × 6 game hours per real hour = 96 world miles per real hour Rule of thumb: ~100 world miles ≈ 1 real hour on foot

Travel Is Movement, Never Teleportation

These multipliers make long-distance travel practical without erasing geography. A character traveling 1,000 miles still physically crosses those 1,000 miles of coordinate world. The journey may be interrupted by terrain, encounters, tracks, weather, other characters or anything else that becomes significant along the route. Magical transportation, if it exists, is a separate future rules question.

Travel speed also remains linked to perception. A character deliberately tracking, searching or moving cautiously sacrifices speed in exchange for greater opportunity to notice subtle features. The baseline rates above describe ordinary purposeful travel, not detailed searching.

Locked travel standard: World time passes at 6× real time and ordinary movement speeds are multiplied by 4×. A baseline 4 mph human walking pace therefore becomes 16 world mph, or approximately 96 world miles traveled per real hour.

Geographical Separation Is Intentional

The world is deliberately large enough for players to leave the original population center and establish genuinely remote homes, settlements and communities. The design should not artificially force remote players back into a central hub merely to increase player density. A group that prefers to roleplay primarily among itself may travel hundreds or thousands of miles away and create its own local community.

At this scale, privacy can arise naturally from geography. A settlement somewhere within a 1,000-mile radius is hidden among roughly four million square miles of possible territory. A systematic half-mile search pattern across a 2,000-by-2,000-mile region would require on the order of eight million miles of travel. Blind searching is therefore impractical; finding a remote settlement should normally depend on information and physical evidence rather than exhaustive geographic sweeps.

“Distance is not a problem to solve. Distance is part of the world.”

Discovery Requires Information

Remote characters do not broadcast their coordinates merely because they are online. Settlements are discovered through things that plausibly reveal them: directions, maps, guides, rumors, maintained roads, repeated wagon traffic, smoke, lights, tracks, trails, discarded objects, altered landscape, or eventually intentional magical means.

Temporary traces should remain temporary. Ordinary footprints, horse tracks and the evidence of a one-time journey may disappear in a day or two under typical conditions, with weather and terrain able to shorten or extend that period. Repeated traffic is different: frequent wagon passage can create persistent ruts, trails and eventually roads. A community that regularly connects itself to civilization therefore becomes easier to find than one that deliberately minimizes traffic.

A determined group can migrate again if its location becomes too well known. Its abandoned settlement remains behind as persistent history while roads deteriorate, fields overgrow, temporary tracks vanish and nature gradually reclaims the site. Future explorers may discover those ruins without knowing where the former residents went.

Group Migration & Offline Passengers

Long journeys should not require every member of a group to remain online simultaneously. With appropriate consent and travel permissions, a character may log out while physically riding as a passenger in a wagon or similar conveyance. The offline character continues to exist at the conveyance's position and moves with it while another player drives.

MIGRATING WAGON Sarah — driving, online Tom — passenger, online Jake — passenger, offline Rachel — passenger, offline The wagon moves through the coordinate world. All passengers remain physically with the wagon. When Jake or Rachel logs in, the character is wherever the wagon has legitimately traveled.

This makes migrations of hundreds or thousands of miles practical for groups whose players cannot all share the same long play session. It must require prior consent or an equivalent trusted-group permission so that offline characters cannot be moved unwillingly.

Remote Wilderness Should Feel Remote

Long journeys may proceed quickly while nothing interesting occurs, then slow or interrupt when something worth playing happens: a trail crosses the route, smoke appears over a ridge, a river blocks the way, an animal is encountered, another active event becomes relevant, or the traveler deliberately begins searching or tracking.

A settlement 10,000 miles from the game's original center should be extraordinarily difficult to locate without useful information. That is intentional. If its residents choose isolation, maintain little traffic and allow their temporary tracks to disappear, geography itself provides meaningful privacy without invisible walls, private instances or artificial safe zones.

“Compress the uneventful. Never compress the interesting.”

14. Roleplay, Communication & Sound

Entity-Targeted Roleplay

Roleplay text may refer to perceived world entities through compact targeting syntax. ~ targets a character, * targets a seen object, and @ refers to the acting character. These tokens are parser handles, not the prose shown to observers. The renderer substitutes the targeted entity's short description.

emote looks over ~blon's shoulder, grinning as his eyes land on *cloth. → A tall, hoary-haired man looks over a tall blonde woman's shoulder, grinning as his eyes land on a bolt of crimson cloth.

Targets must resolve through the normal perception/entity system. A character cannot use roleplay targeting to reveal an object or person the character has not perceived.

Parenthetical Emotes on Commands

Any appropriate character action may carry parenthetical roleplay describing how the action is performed. The mechanical command still determines what actually happens; the parenthetical text adds player-authored description and cannot dictate a mechanical outcome.

hit salmon (grunting heavily as he attacks) → You try to hit a salmon-haired young man, grunting heavily as you attack.

This syntax should be handled by the common command parser rather than independently reimplemented by combat, movement, object and communication commands.

Speech Family

SAY, TELL, SHOUT, WHISPER, SCREAM and SING are forms of the same underlying speech system. They differ in such properties as direction, volume and audible range. Speech commands may freely alternate spoken text with parenthetical emote segments.

shout (waves his hands in the air) I hate this place! (trips on a loose board, cursing) See! It's falling apart! (plops to the ground, tending his now-bleeding toe.) → A tall, hoary-haired man waves his hands in the air, "I hate this place!" He trips on a loose board, cursing, "See! It's falling apart!" He plops to the ground, tending his now-bleeding toe.

EMOTE itself is reserved for nonverbal roleplay rather than spoken dialogue. Ordinary physical sounds such as laughing, coughing, sighing or grunting may still be described by an emote; language-bearing communication belongs to the speech family.

Languages Are Binary

Language knowledge is binary. A character either knows a language and understands it completely, or does not know it and receives no mangled partial-language text. The system may distinguish a recognized language from an unfamiliar one, but there are no percentage language skills and no progressive gobbledygook.

Because speech and physical emote segments remain distinct, a listener who cannot understand the language may still perceive the accompanying gestures and actions. Hearing, language comprehension and visual perception are separate questions.

Player Voice Is Preserved

Verbosity and accessibility settings may shorten, aggregate or expand system-generated narration, but they never abbreviate, summarize or rewrite another player's communication or emote. If a character perceives player-authored roleplay, the authored portion is delivered intact, subject only to legitimate perception such as hearing, language and visibility.

System messages may be aggressively aggregated when useful. For example, a group of ten followers crossing an area need not generate ten repetitive arrival or departure notices. This is particularly important for players using screen readers.

“Verbosity changes the game's narration, never the players' voices.”

Accessibility

The complete game should remain playable through text and keyboard alone. The browser client should be friendly to established screen readers through semantic HTML and appropriate live-region behavior, while optional built-in text-to-speech may be offered as a convenience rather than a replacement for a player's preferred accessibility tools. Visual styling, color and audio cues may reinforce information but must never be the only way to obtain essential information or perform an action.

Sound Has Physical Range

Speech and other noises are physical events originating at world coordinates. Central setup/configuration data should define tunable base hearing radii and related sound properties for speech forms such as whisper, say/tell, sing, shout and scream, as well as environmental sounds such as doors, galloping horses, falling trees, bells, gunshots or explosions if those sounds exist in the setting. Exact distances remain playtest values rather than hard-coded command rules.

Distance, Spaces, portals and environment may modify what a listener receives. A nearby listener may understand the actual words; a more distant listener may merely hear shouting; a wall or closed door may muffle or block sound. Large sounds can be perceived at greater distances without requiring distant empty terrain to be actively simulated.

The sound system should therefore produce structured events with an origin, sound type and configured range, then allow the perception system to determine whether each potential listener hears it, how clearly, from what direction, and—when language is involved—whether the words are understood.

Sound rule: Commands produce configured sound events; the perception system decides what each listener can actually hear.

16. Objects, Stacking & Samples

Capabilities Instead of Rigid Object Types

Objects should not use a fixed array of generic value slots whose meanings change according to a rigid object type. Universal fields such as keywords, short description, long description, full description and weight should retain one meaning everywhere. Specialized behavior is attached through named capabilities/components with clearly named properties.

SWORD object: keywords, descriptions, weight weapon: wielding properties, wound ceiling OAK TABLE object: keywords, descriptions, weight seating: seats = 4 WAGON object seating container vehicle

An object may possess any sensible combination of capabilities. A storage bench can be both seating and a container; a wagon can be seating, storage and a vehicle. Components describe specialized behavior rather than forcing an object into one exclusive type.

“An object is not a type. An object has capabilities.”

Improvised Wielding

Physical objects do not need an explicit Weapon capability merely to be swung at another character. If a character can reasonably wield an object that lacks defined weapon properties, combat may treat it under generic improvised-object rules. Purpose-built weapons carry explicit weapon properties; ordinary tables, belts, stools, mugs and similar objects remain ordinary objects until a player decides to use one creatively.

wield leather belt → You wield a worn leather belt. A table may still provide four seats while also being wielded, swung or used to parry if the physical rules permit it.

This favors general physical rules over proliferating special object types such as weaponized tables or improvised-weapon belts.

Stacking

Identical discrete objects automatically stack when they occupy the same inventory, container or location. The stack is represented internally as a single object with a quantity/count rather than one database object per copy.

a china plate (x7)

The seven plates remain conceptually seven discrete plates. Taking or consuming one decrements the count; any change that makes an individual plate non-identical causes that object to split from the stack. Material, color, condition, inscriptions and other meaningful state therefore participate in stack compatibility.

Sampled Objects

Sampled objects remain useful for continuous divisible materials, while stacks represent separate interchangeable things. A bolt of linen may hold twelve yards, a spool of thread a number of lengths, and a liquid container a number of sips. Crafts consume the required amount from the sample atomically.

STACK SAMPLE a china plate (x7) bolt of linen 7 discrete plates 12 / 12 yards an iron nail (x40) spool of thread 40 discrete nails 20 / 20 lengths waterskin 8 / 8 sips

Look may describe a sample qualitatively as full, nearly full, partially used or nearly depleted, while examining it may reveal the remaining quantity more precisely.

Full Samples May Stack

A sampled object may stack only while it is completely full. Thus a bolt of linen (x12) unambiguously means twelve complete bolts. If each full bolt contains twelve yards, the stack represents 144 yards. When a Craft consumes from one member of the stack, one full sample splits away and becomes the partial object while the remaining full samples stay stacked.

Before: a bolt of linen (x12) each bolt = 12 / 12 yards Craft consumes 1 yard: After: a bolt of linen (x11) a nearly full bolt of linen = 11 / 12 yards

Partial samples do not auto-stack. Compatible partial samples may support an explicit combine operation when physically sensible and when the result does not exceed the sample maximum. The exact compatibility rules can remain simple and material-specific.

Prototype Versus Instance State

Prototypes define what all examples of an object share: descriptions, normal weight and available capabilities. Individual instances store only state that can vary, such as remaining sample quantity, stack count, color, material, condition or an inscription. This avoids multiplying prototypes merely to represent cosmetic variations.

A shirt prototype can therefore become linen, cotton, wool or silk through keyed crafting and later take one of several dye colors while remaining the same fundamental object design.

Prefer transformations and instance state over multiplying prototypes.

System Direction

The intended server architecture is a browser-based JavaScript client connected by WebSocket to a Python game server. The Python process owns the live world and Crown & Call rules. A relational database such as PostgreSQL stores persistent accounts, characters and world changes. A reverse proxy such as Nginx can handle public HTTPS/WebSocket traffic.

Live activity should generally operate on in-memory state. Persistence should be transactional. Empty wilderness should remain dormant. The architecture should be built outward from a small playable world rather than beginning with a giant general-purpose MUD engine.

Design test: If Jake cuts down a tree, builds a cabin, abandons it, and nobody returns for twenty years, the engine should be able to tell the next visitor what remains—without having spent twenty years simulating the empty clearing.

Recovered Locked Design Decisions

Geographic Naming

Named geographic features display their names automatically; players do not need to learn a name first. Roads may remain unnamed. Duplicate geographic names are prohibited within 500 miles. Admin reviewers see possible conflicts and can jump to the conflicting feature. Pending proposals are completely invisible to ordinary players. Admins may permanently edit names, manually name unnamed features, and remove a feature's name/identity without altering the underlying terrain. Admin-created names follow the same conflict rules. Previous names are retained as admin-only historical metadata. Even minor landmarks may be named.

The submitting account/character is tracked. An account may submit one proposal every fixed 30 days, beginning at submission regardless of approval or rejection; cooldowns cannot be banked and pending proposals cannot be withdrawn. A character needs at least 31 total CAP points to submit. Competing proposals are allowed; the first approved name closes the others without refunding their cooldowns. Publicly approved names do not identify the submitter, but admins can see the submitter. Naming statistics and complete submission history are permanent. Naming-specific bans are superseded by COMM-BLOCK.

Player Pfile & Admin Notes

Authorized admins may append positive, negative, informational, or warning notes to a player's account/pfile. Notes are visible to admins and the affected player, permanent, and append-only; corrections require a new note. Each note permits exactly one player response unless COMM-BLOCKED. There are no reply threads; further staff response requires another NOTE or WARN. Players may respond to NOTE, WARN, COMM-BLOCK, and GAME-BAN entries unless COMM-BLOCKED. A GAME-BANNED player may authenticate to a restricted account interface, read history, and make the permitted response, but cannot enter the world.

NOTES twpaige NOTES Nimrod READ twpaige 22 READ Nimrod 22 NOTE Nimrod <title> WARN Nimrod <title> RESPOND Nimrod 22 SEARCH NOTES <text> NOTES ADMIN <admin>

NOTES lists newest first with number, date, type, admin and title. NOTE/WARN and RESPOND use the multiline editor. Moderation actions commit only after editor completion. A player response is permanent, tied to its entry, and generates admin NEWS. SEARCH NOTES searches titles, bodies and player responses across pfiles and returns note ID, player/character reference, date, type, admin, title and match location. Note IDs are stable and permanent within the pfile.

COMM-BLOCK

COMM-BLOCK is account-level and blocks OOC communication and staff-facing contribution systems while allowing ordinary play and IC communication. It blocks OOC channels, OOC tells/DMs, OOC boards/forums, responses to admin notes, geographic naming and similar future OOC systems. IC say, whisper, shout, character-written notes/letters and ordinary gameplay remain available. Deliberately using IC systems to evade a COMM-BLOCK may lead to GAME-BAN and this rule should be plainly published.

Existing history remains visible. A COMM-BLOCK has a player-visible reason, start and expiration timestamps, original duration and live remaining duration. Very long durations may function as permanent. A newer restriction takes precedence; there is no separate extend operation. Manual early removal is logged; passive expiration is not. Expiration is determined lazily from timestamps. Applying a block writes a permanent pfile entry and admin NEWS.

GAME-BAN & Moderation Queries

GAME-BAN is account-level and prevents entering the game world. It stores a player-visible reason, start, expiration and duration. A banned player retains restricted account access to history and the permitted response unless COMM-BLOCKED. Passive expiration creates no record and is determined lazily. A new ban replaces the currently effective restriction while old records remain immutable. New bans create pfile entries and NEWS; manual early removal is separately logged. The conceptual progression is NORMAL → COMM-BLOCK → GAME-BAN, with admin discretion rather than mandatory escalation.

BANS BANS ACTIVE BANS EXPIRED BANS Nimrod BANS ADMIN <admin> Equivalent query structures apply to COMM-BLOCKS and WARNS ADMIN <admin>.

These are indexed database queries/views, not duplicate physical files. Active/expired state and remaining time are computed on demand. The explicit ADMIN keyword avoids ambiguity.

Admin NEWS, Permissions & Succession

Every new pfile moderation action generates generic admin NEWS; passive expiration does not. Each admin has an individual last-read position. READ NEW NEWS shows unread entries; VIEW NEWS 12 begins sequential viewing from entry 12. NEWS is a virtual administrative board.

Every administrative capability has its own permission. Admins see help and commands only for capabilities they possess. Reusable role templates may bundle permissions, but assignment copies permissions rather than creating a live dependency. Vboard access is individually permission-controlled. Admin levels range 1–100: level establishes hierarchy while permissions establish actual capabilities. An admin may administer only lower-level admins. Only level 99+ may set levels, still subject to hierarchy. Level 100 is LEAD ADMIN and normally only one is active, though temporary transition overlap is possible. A Level 100 may voluntarily lower their own level.

Admins level 75–99 may file one pending Level-100 succession petition. It grants no immediate authority and waits 30 days. The process is loudly and permanently audited. Any existing Level 100 may veto it. Any existing Level-100 login during the waiting period automatically cancels it with prejudice. No scheduler is needed; resolution occurs lazily on the next relevant interaction after 30 days. A successful petitioner becomes active Level 100. The previous Level-100 account remains historically Level 100 but is locked against modification by others. Identity recovery/SMS are deferred.

Physical Boards & Vboards

Ordinary boards are physical world objects and geographically constrained; a room object such as “a group of gossiping patrons” may expose a board. Normally the character must be physically present to read or post. Multiple physical objects may share one message pool, while each object exposes exactly one pool. Clan restrictions support multiple clans with independent minimum READ and POST ranks.

NOTES commoners READ commoners 2322 POST commoners <title> NOTES VBOARD building READ VBOARD building 22 POST VBOARD building <title>

Admin status alone grants no board access. Positive permissions are READ, POST, MODERATE and CONFIGURE; none implies another, though templates may bundle them. Physical presence remains relevant unless a future explicit remote capability is added. Deletion is soft deletion. Original post, author, timestamps, deleting/restoring actor and times are retained. MODERATE admins may restore posts. Players may soft-delete their own posts. Posts are immutable after submission; corrections require new posts. There are no threads. Messages persist until soft-deleted. Message numbers are permanent, never reused, unique only within the board pool, and each board starts at 1. Deleted numbers remain gaps.

VBOARD is an explicit namespace independent of physical room objects. Vboards are individually permission-controlled; inaccessible vboards do not appear in listings/help. READ and POST remain separate permissions.

Pager, Settings & Aliases

CCMud uses one standard pager for lengthy output. Default page length is 20 lines. Enter advances; Q quits; there is no backward paging. The prompt is [Enter to continue, Q to quit]. Page length is an account-level preference with sensible bounds to be determined later.

SETTINGS displays only player-adjustable settings and current values. Changes such as SETTINGS PAGELENGTH 30 apply immediately and auto-save; there is no SAVE command. Setting names are strict/canonical. Commands may have explicit shortcuts but never arbitrary prefix matching. L=LOOK, I=INVENTORY, and explicit movement aliases N/S/E/W/NE/NW/SE/SW/U/D are reserved; full direction words also work.

Coordinate Travel

GO is the general travel command. GO NORTH, GO N, NORTH and N are equivalent forms. GO CABIN travels toward a visible object's actual coordinates/bearing rather than rounding to a compass point. GO 135 travels an exact numeric heading. The world supports headings 0–359°. General descriptive navigation may use the 16-point compass: N, NNE, NE, ENE, E, ESE, SE, SSE, S, SSW, SW, WSW, W, WNW, NW, NNW. Numeric-heading travel requires an accessible compass; ordinary compass-point travel does not.

GO <numeric> holds the absolute heading until stopped, obstructed or changed. Travel is continuous coordinate movement, not room jumping. Normal commands do not halt travel. A moving character can DROP COIN at the coordinates occupied at that moment and continue traveling.

Travel Observation

While traveling, the server performs a small/local meaningful-visibility-change check every 5 seconds, not a full LOOK. Nothing prints when nothing meaningful changes. Fixed sampling naturally makes faster travel less observant because more ground is crossed between samples; no arbitrary perception roll is needed merely to recreate that effect. If no meaningful observation occurs for 30 seconds, print a heartbeat such as “You continue traveling north.” or “You continue traveling at 037° (NE).” Meaningful observations and player LOOK/SCAN reset the heartbeat, while five-second local checks continue normally.

Other characters/creatures are meaningful observations except entities already traveling with the character, such as followers, the followed character, or group members. Once a meaningful entity catches attention, relevant changes involving it may continue to be reported while pertinent; it is not instantly forgotten after the first appearance.

Maps, Bookmarks & Compass

All maps use one universal world coordinate/map system; bookmarks may refer anywhere, including other continents. A physical map is limited by marks, not geographic coverage, and holds at most 200 bookmarks. More marks require another map. Bookmark names are unique within a map and may be deleted. Raw X/Y coordinates remain internal. Bookmarks belong to the physical map, not abstract account knowledge.

Both maps must be physically accessible to copy a bookmark. Copying is perfectly accurate by default and does not introduce CAP/skill error. Exact coordinates copy while the receiving bookmark may be renamed. This supports military maps, treasure maps, quest maps and courier/carrier-pigeon delivery. A character may mark the current location or an estimated remote location using bearing plus distance, conceptually BOOKMARK OrcCamp 037 3.5. Prefer player estimation error to arbitrary system-generated cartographic error.

A compass reading to a visible target reports exact numeric bearing plus nearest 16-point direction, e.g. 037° (NE). COMPASS <bookmark> calculates bearing from current position to an accessible bookmark; compass alone does not provide distance. A map itself can calculate both current distance and heading for one explicitly examined bookmark, e.g. EXAMINE MAP ORCCAMP → “OrcCamp lies 3.6 miles away at 214° (SW).” Do not calculate all 200 marks on ordinary map look. There is no magical GO HOME/autopilot; players navigate manually and re-check after obstacles/course changes.

WATCH

WATCH <target> supports one active target; a new WATCH replaces the old one. WATCH ends immediately when sight is lost and never auto-reacquires. It works while stationary or traveling and uses the same five-second cadence. Its purpose is to expose movement information useful for estimating destination/interception. Maintaining WATCH longer does not artificially improve accuracy.

Every five seconds while visible, WATCH reports the target's current movement direction and actual selected movement speed even if unchanged. Direction is intentionally only the 8-point compass (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW): no numeric heading and no 16-point precision. Speed is the target's actual movement setting, e.g. WALK, JOG, RUN or SPRINT, not an observer estimate. WATCH reports movement data only. Speech/hearing remains entirely in the speech/hearing system; emotes/actions remain entirely in normal visibility. WATCH neither adds nor suppresses them.

Movement Speed & Stamina

Heading and speed are independent persistent travel states. Example: SPEED JOG followed by GO NE continues northeast at JOG until direction changes, speed changes, STOP is issued, an obstacle/stopping event occurs, or stamina is exhausted. On stamina exhaustion the character comes to a complete stop and the active movement command is canceled. There is no automatic SPRINT → RUN → JOG → WALK downgrade; the player must deliberately begin traveling again.

Movement speed order: TRUDGE WALK JOG RUN SPRINT

TRUDGE and WALK normally consume no stamina and are indefinitely sustainable. Severe overloading may make even these low-speed modes consume stamina. Faster movement modes consume stamina according to the stamina system; exact rates remain to be defined.

Recovered Design Character

Across these decisions: prefer simple deterministic systems over unnecessary calculations; prefer timestamp/lazy evaluation over background scheduler jobs; use indexed database queries/views rather than duplicate derived files; make physical geography and coordinate position matter; let physical objects such as maps carry knowledge; let continuous movement create emergent gameplay; execute travel-time actions at the character's actual current coordinates; allow fixed observation sampling to create the natural speed-versus-awareness tradeoff; avoid artificial mechanics when physical simulation already creates the desired effect; and keep systems decoupled so WATCH handles movement observation, speech handles hearing, and emote/action visibility handles visible actions.

World Generation Requirements Interview — Questions 122–218

Status: Locked decisions from the world-generation design interview. The entries preserve the substance of the questions, Thomas's answers, follow-up clarification, and the design reasoning developed in the conversation. Where a later answer revised an earlier one, the later decision controls.

Recurring design rule: Generate causes and derive consequences. Keep systems physically grounded, but abstract detail that does not earn its gameplay complexity. Skill and knowledge improve what a character can notice or understand; they should not become magical radar.

Question 122 — Surface clues for buried mineral deposits

Question: Should buried mineral deposits sometimes produce subtle surface clues—unusual rock fragments, coloration, mineralized outcrops, and similar evidence—that a skilled prospector could recognize?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Buried deposits can create subtle, physically plausible surface clues. Prospecting remains a physical search: skill improves what a character notices and understands rather than acting as hidden-resource radar.

Question 123 — Mineral resource quality

Question: Should a mineral vein have a simple quality/richness property as well as physical size?

Answer & locked decision: Yes, keep it simple. Deposits use broad richness classifications such as poor, ordinary, rich, and exceptional rather than ore-concentration percentages.

Question 124 — Gemstone distribution

Question: Should gemstones be tied to larger ore deposits or occur separately?

Answer & locked decision: Scattered. Gemstones are rare scattered resources. Geology can influence where a gem is plausible, but gems may occur as isolated pockets or individual discoveries rather than always belonging to an ore vein.

Question 125 — Gemstone richness

Question: Should gemstone occurrences use detailed carats, purity, and crystal-density geology?

Answer & locked decision: No; keep it simple. Generated gemstone occurrences use a simple size/richness classification. Individual recovered gems can later be handled by the item/crafting system.

Question 126 — Resource catalog

Question: Should natural resources come from a fixed catalog or be procedurally invented?

Answer & locked decision: Fixed catalog. The generator controls location, distribution, abundance, richness, and exposure, but never invents new woods, ores, stones, gems, or other resource types.

Question 127 — Base resource rarity

Question: Should each catalog resource have a predefined base rarity that geography modifies?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Each resource has a base rarity. Local geology, terrain, climate where relevant, and regional modifiers such as the Origin resource band modify actual occurrence without making rare materials casually common.

Question 128 — Resource identification

Question: Does physical discovery automatically identify an unfamiliar ore, stone, mineral, or gem?

Answer & locked decision: Knowledge required. A character without appropriate knowledge receives a physical description; knowledgeable characters can identify the material. Perception does not automatically equal knowledge.

Question 129 — Depletion feedback

Question: Should players receive physical indications that a deposit is thinning rather than mechanical percentages?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Depletion is communicated descriptively: veins narrow, ore becomes sparse, ordinary rock increasingly replaces valuable material, and similar observable signs. The engine may know exact quantities; the character does not.

Question 130 — Ordinary surface stone

Question: Should loose stone and fieldstone be generally available wherever local geology supports it without individual deposits?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Ordinary stone can be gathered from suitable terrain. Serious quarrying, specialized stone, and valuable geological materials require exposed bedrock or actual deposits.

Question 131 — Clay and sand

Question: Should clay and sand be derived from suitable terrain rather than generated as thousands of individual deposits?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Clay and sand are derived resources available from appropriate riverbanks, soils, beaches, bars, sedimentary areas, and similar geography.

Question 132 — Salt scarcity

Question: Should salt be a geographically constrained and potentially valuable resource?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Mineable salt deposits and rare salt springs can occur where appropriate, allowing inland salt sources to become economically important.

Question 133 — Salt from seawater

Question: Can players produce salt from seawater by evaporation?

Answer & locked decision: Sure. Seawater evaporation can produce salt, but it requires meaningful time, labor, suitable conditions, and infrastructure, so inland natural sources retain substantial value.

Question 134 — Fertile farmland scarcity

Question: Should especially fertile farmland be deliberately rare enough to drive economic competition?

Answer & locked decision: No. Soil quality may vary naturally, but the generator should not manufacture agricultural scarcity merely to force competition over prime farmland.

Question 135 — Freshwater abundance

Question: Should freshwater generally be plentiful in habitable regions?

Answer & locked decision: Make it plentiful. Rivers, streams, lakes, springs, and other freshwater should be reasonably obtainable in habitable country; genuinely arid regions remain the natural exception.

Question 136 — Wild edible plants

Question: Should berries, nuts, roots, fruits, mushrooms, and other edible wild plants be plentiful in suitable habitat?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Wild edible plants are fairly plentiful where habitat supports them, with availability and type influenced by vegetation, climate, terrain, and season.

Question 137 — Poisonous plants and mushrooms

Question: Should harmful plants and mushrooms exist and make identification knowledge useful?

Answer & locked decision: Yes, but don't kill anyone with it. Mistakes can cause illness, impairment, vomiting, temporary conditions, and similar consequences, but an innocent foraging error should not produce character death.

Question 138 — Medicinal plants

Question: Should wild medicinal plants exist, and how should their properties be recognized?

Answer & locked decision: Knowledge. Medicinal plants exist, but appropriate knowledge is required to identify useful properties and understand preparation or use.

Question 139 — Seasonal vegetation

Question: Should seasons meaningfully change what wild foods and plants can be harvested?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Fruits, berries, nuts, herbs, mushrooms, and similar resources have meaningful seasonal availability without simulating individual plant life cycles.

Question 140 — Winter forage

Question: Should temperate winter substantially reduce wild plant-food availability?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Winter makes stored food, hunting, fishing, trade, or movement toward warmer country more important.

Question 141 — Growing season complexity

Question: Should agriculture use a simple regional growing season instead of detailed crop simulation?

Answer & locked decision: Simple. Climate, season, and basic land suitability determine whether agriculture can proceed, without detailed rainfall, temperature, soil chemistry, or growth simulation.

Question 142 — Crop-specific climate geography

Question: Should different crops have distinct broad climate preferences?

Answer & locked decision: No. Crops do not receive separate regional climate-preference systems. Agriculture stays deliberately simple: suitable growing season plus suitable land.

Question 143 — Generated agriculture

Question: Should natural world generation stop at farmable land, leaving fields and cultivation to inhabitants?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Crops, fields, orchards, fences, irrigation, cleared acreage, and other agriculture exist only when created by players or later NPC civilizations.

Question 144 — Wild fruit and nut trees

Question: Can fruit and nut trees occur naturally even though orchards are constructed?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Wild fruit and nut trees can occur naturally; organized orchards are cultivated features.

Question 145 — Regional plant distribution

Question: Should wild plant species vary by habitat and region so distant travel can reveal unfamiliar plants?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Plant distribution is regional as well as habitat-based, giving exploration botanical value while retaining the fixed resource/species catalog.

Question 146 — Wood species mechanics

Question: Should different tree species produce mechanically different wood?

Answer & locked decision: No. Wood is wood. Tree species can provide environmental flavor and regional identity, but harvested wood is one basic mechanical resource.

Question 147 — Stone type mechanics

Question: Should granite, limestone, sandstone, and similar building stones have distinct crafting mechanics?

Answer & locked decision: Stone is stone. Rock types still matter to geology, caves, cliffs, mineral occurrence, appearance, and erosion, but harvested ordinary building stone is one mechanical resource.

Question 148 — Distinct metals

Question: Should metals remain mechanically distinct?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Iron, copper, tin, gold, silver, and other metals remain distinct because their differences matter to mining, refining, smithing, crafting, and trade.

Question 149 — Ore processing scope

Question: Should world generation model smelting, refining, alloying, and metal yields?

Answer & locked decision: No. World generation handles where ore exists, its physical extent, and simple richness. Processing belongs to the later crafting system.

Question 150 — Coal

Question: Should coal remain a distinct mineable resource rather than generic fuel?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Coal is a distinct geological resource and can later matter to smithing, crafting, and industry; wood and charcoal remain separate possible fuels.

Question 151 — Compact resource categories

Question: Should the resource system abstract distinctions that do not create meaningful gameplay?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Differentiate resources only when the distinction earns its complexity. Metals, gems, coal, salt, and special resources can remain distinct while ordinary wood, stone, sand, clay, and similar materials stay broad where practical.

Question 152 — Rare special materials

Question: May the fixed catalog include a small number of exceptionally rare natural materials for high-end crafting or magic?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Such materials are deliberately authored, never procedurally invented, and still obey physical geography and normal discovery.

Question 153 — World-unique deposits

Question: Should rare special materials be so scarce that only one or a handful of deposits exist worldwide?

Answer & locked decision: Not that unique. They may be very uncommon, but the enormous world should retain a reasonable possibility of another source being discovered.

Question 154 — Regional resource concentrations

Question: Can particular geological regions or continents be unusually rich in certain resources?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Strong regional concentrations can create natural trade geography, but resources are not exclusively confined to a single continent or region.

Question 155 — Additional fossil fuels

Question: Should peat, petroleum, natural gas, or similar fuels be modeled?

Answer & locked decision: No. Coal is the only geological fuel resource that needs to be modeled.

Question 156 — Depleted mines

Question: Should the excavation remain after a mineral deposit is exhausted?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Empty mines persist as world history and can later be discovered, explored, occupied, modified, reused, or built within.

Question 157 — Mine reclamation

Question: Should nature reclaim surface evidence of abandoned mines while underground excavations persist?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Entrances, spoil piles, cleared work areas, and similar surface traces can become overgrown, while underground geometry remains essentially intact unless modified.

Question 158 — Forgotten mine entrances

Question: Can reclamation make old mine entrances nearly invisible?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Casual travelers may miss them; deliberate searching or appropriate knowledge can reveal subtle signs of old excavation.

Question 159 — General reclamation of construction

Question: Should reclamation apply to abandoned cabins, roads, fields, quarries, camps, and settlements?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Human-made features become increasingly overgrown and harder to recognize while durable physical remains persist. Reclamation is not deletion.

Question 160 — Structural decay

Question: Should abandoned wooden construction decay and stonework generally last longer?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Wooden structures deteriorate and can collapse or disappear without maintenance; stone construction usually survives much longer.

Question 161 — Metal decay

Question: Should ordinary iron and steel rust while precious metals persist much longer?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Material type affects long-term survival without requiring detailed corrosion simulation.

Question 162 — Use equals maintenance

Question: Should active use prevent routine decay without repair micromanagement?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Use = maintenance. Regularly used structures and objects are assumed to receive ordinary upkeep automatically; significant neglect is what permits decay.

Question 163 — What counts as use

Question: Should maintenance be judged at a sensible property/structure level rather than object by object?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Meaningful ongoing occupancy or activity maintains the place as a whole; players do not need to touch every door, bed, wall, or fence.

Question 164 — Abandonment grace period

Question: Should decay wait through a generous period of non-use?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Visible abandonment and decay operate on the scale of world months or years, not days, with material and environment affecting eventual rate.

Question 165 — Decay stages

Question: Should structures pass through recognizable stages rather than instantly switching from building to gone?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Use simple stages such as maintained → neglected → deteriorating → ruined → remnants, with visible consequences derived from materials and construction.

Question 166 — Restoring ruins

Question: Can players restore old ruins rather than demolishing and rebuilding?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Surviving foundations, walls, structural elements, and usable material can reduce labor and resources compared with starting from nothing.

Question 167 — Salvage

Question: Can abandoned or ruined structures be salvaged for materials?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Recoverable material generally declines as decay advances, while durable materials may remain useful much longer.

Question 168 — Abandonment versus ownership

Question: Does physical abandonment or ruin automatically erase legal ownership?

Answer & locked decision: No. They may squat, but it is not theirs. Occupancy and physical condition are separate from legal/claim ownership.

Question 169 — Squatter interaction

Question: Can squatters use and work on abandoned property they do not own?

Answer & locked decision: Yes, subject to ownership protections clarified immediately afterward. Squatters may occupy and use abandoned property, and may maintain or repair it, but ownership does not transfer.

Question 170 — Destruction of owned property

Question: Can players damage or destroy another player's protected property simply because the world is physically simulated?

Answer & locked decision: No. Ownership creates protection against destructive actions; the game does not permit ordinary players to damage, dismantle, salvage, or destroy another player's protected property.

Question 171 — Helpful squatter repairs

Question: May squatters repair or maintain someone else's abandoned property?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Helpful maintenance and repair are allowed, but they grant no ownership rights and do not permit destructive or fundamental alteration.

Question 172 — Theft from protected property

Question: Can players steal stored movable items from protected property?

Answer & locked decision: No theft. Ownership protection covers properly stored possessions as well as the structure itself.

Question 173 — Dropped wilderness items

Question: Does ownership protection apply to things simply dropped or abandoned in unprotected wilderness?

Answer & locked decision: No. Correct: protection applies to properly stored or placed items within protected property. Wilderness drops can be discovered and taken.

Question 174 — Wilderness caches

Question: Can players hide or bury goods in the wilderness and receive ownership protection?

Answer & locked decision: They may cache, but it is fair game for thieves. Concealment is the defense; a discovered wilderness cache is not ownership-protected.

Question 175 — Discovering caches

Question: Should hidden caches be found through physical searching rather than a detection radius?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Physical clues, deliberate searching, and appropriate skill/knowledge govern discovery; there is no generic hidden-object radar.

Question 176 — Cache clues fading

Question: Should evidence of a buried cache fade with world time?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Soil settles, vegetation returns, tracks vanish, and an old cache can eventually become extremely difficult to notice.

Question 177 — Cached-item decay

Question: Should cached items age according to storage quality and environment?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Dry, sealed, sheltered storage protects better than wet soil or exposure; this can remain a simple environmental modifier.

Question 178 — Container protection classes

Question: Should containers use a few simple protection classes rather than detailed preservation physics?

Answer & locked decision: Simple classes. Broad states such as open, covered, or sealed can determine how well contents resist environmental decay.

Question 179 — Food spoilage

Question: Should perishable food spoil?

Answer & locked decision: No spoilage if properly stored. Proper storage removes routine spoilage bookkeeping; exposed or abandoned food may still deteriorate.

Question 180 — Proper food storage

Question: Should any normal food-storage container in a maintained property count as proper storage?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. No micromanagement of salting, drying, smoking, refrigeration, humidity, or preservation timers is required.

Question 181 — Food while traveling

Question: Is food in ordinary carried inventory automatically protected from spoilage?

Answer & locked decision: No, not if it is not in a storage container. Appropriate containers protect traveling provisions; loose food can deteriorate.

Question 182 — Spoilage states

Question: How detailed should improperly stored food spoilage be?

Answer & locked decision: Simple states, then purge. Use broad freshness states and automatically remove fully spoiled food rather than accumulating rotten objects forever.

Question 183 — Purging transient clutter

Question: Should worthless scraps and trivial discarded debris eventually purge?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Transient clutter can decay and disappear so the persistent world does not accumulate millions of meaningless objects; meaningful objects and historical physical changes follow their own rules.

Question 184 — Historical significance and immortality

Question: Should the engine automatically preserve an ordinary item because it became historically significant?

Answer & locked decision: No. History does not make an object magically immortal. Players who want an item preserved must store or maintain it appropriately.

Question 185 — Dropped durable valuables

Question: Should valuable durable items persist longer in the wilderness than trivial clutter?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. A gold ring, sword, tool, and similar object can remain much longer according to material, while remaining unprotected and available to whoever finds it.

Question 186 — Long-term buried objects

Question: Can deliberately buried durable objects survive for many years or decades?

Answer & locked decision: Only if they are in an appropriate vessel. Burial by itself does not grant preservation; container and conditions matter.

Question 187 — Container failure

Question: Should abandoned containers eventually deteriorate and expose their contents?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Containers age according to material and environment. When the vessel fails, contents lose its protection and follow the rules for their new exposure.

Question 188 — Maintained containers

Question: Does use = maintenance also apply to barrels, chests, sacks, cupboards, and similar storage in active properties?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Actively used storage is assumed maintained; players do not replace ordinary containers merely because calendar time passed.

Question 189 — Wilderness shelters as property

Question: Can a lean-to, hunting shack, remote cabin, or similar isolated shelter be a maintainable property?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. It need not belong to a settlement or formal homestead; periodic meaningful use can maintain it.

Question 190 — Temporary campsites

Question: Is an ordinary campsite a maintained property?

Answer & locked decision: No. Correct: a normal camp is temporary and can leave fading physical traces. Persistent construction is what can turn a location into maintainable property.

Question 191 — Repeated camping

Question: Can repeated use of the same unbuilt campsite create visible persistent traces?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Worn ground, an established fire area, disturbed vegetation, and faint approach traces can emerge, while the site remains unowned absent a separate claim.

Question 192 — Emergent trails

Question: Can repeated foot, horse, wagon, or other travel wear trails into the landscape?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Repeated travel naturally creates increasingly obvious paths; heavier and more frequent traffic produces stronger trails.

Question 193 — Trail reclamation

Question: Do unused trails fade?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Light paths disappear relatively quickly, heavily established routes take longer, continued traffic reinforces them, and neglect lets nature reclaim them.

Question 194 — Constructed roads

Question: Does a heavily worn trail automatically become a road?

Answer & locked decision: No. A true road requires deliberate construction or improvement such as clearing, grading, drainage, bridging, or surfacing.

Question 195 — Road/trail speed bonus

Question: Should trails and roads provide an inherent mechanical movement-speed bonus?

Answer & locked decision: No. Their value is navigation, recognizable routing, connections, and potentially choosing easier terrain—not a speed multiplier.

Question 196 — Navigation on routes

Question: Should roads and trails be easy to follow without granting extra speed?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. A player can issue natural route-oriented travel such as “walk north road”; the route's actual geometry determines the character's path.

Question 197 — Following named routes

Question: Can a character follow a known named road or trail continuously?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Commands such as “follow King's Road” can continue along real route geometry until stopped, obstructed, reaching a meaningful junction, or interrupted by something important.

Question 198 — Generated road names

Question: Should roads and trails have names by default?

Answer & locked decision: No. They begin unnamed. Names arise only through players or NPC societies; the world generator does not label them.

Question 199 — Generated natural landmark names

Question: Should rivers, mountains, lakes, caves, forests, islands, and other natural geography have intrinsic generated names?

Answer & locked decision: No. Natural features simply exist until people name them.

Question 200 — Competing canonical names

Question: Can the same feature maintain different names for different groups?

Answer & locked decision: No. Once named, a feature has one canonical name rather than faction-specific aliases.

Question 201 — Who names geography

Question: Who gets to establish the canonical name of an unnamed feature?

Answer & locked decision: The first player may propose a name, but it must be approved by an admin before becoming canonical.

Question 202 — Pending name reservation

Question: Does the first pending proposal reserve the feature?

Answer & locked decision: No. Competing names are allowed until one is approved. Approval establishes the canonical name and closes the naming question.

Question 203 — Renaming approved geography

Question: Can ordinary players later rename an approved feature?

Answer & locked decision: No. Approved names are permanent for ordinary players; only an admin can rename or remove them.

Question 204 — Minor landmarks

Question: Can small persistent features be named, or only major geography?

Answer & locked decision: Include minor landmarks. Springs, clearings, rock formations, cave chambers, creek bends, small islands, and similar recognizable features can receive approved names.

Question 205 — Naming player-created landmarks

Question: Can bridges, roads, mines, farms, buildings, settlements, and other constructed landmarks be named?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Player-created persistent landmarks use the same canonical naming concept.

Question 206 — Approval for creator names

Question: Does a player automatically get to name something they personally built?

Answer & locked decision: No. Yes to approval: names for player-created landmarks and property still require admin approval.

Question 207 — Name visibility — revised

Question: Should players have to learn an approved name from a map, character, sign, or document before seeing it?

Answer & locked decision: This was initially answered “Learn from map or char,” but Thomas immediately backed up and revised the rule: just always display the name. The revised decision supersedes the first answer. Once approved, a canonical name is displayed to everyone who perceives the feature.

Question 208 — Unnamed feature display

Question: How should a feature be shown before it receives an approved canonical name?

Answer & locked decision: Use a straightforward type label such as “Unnamed Road” rather than inventing a pseudo-name. The same pattern can apply to other namable feature types.

Question 209 — Duplicate names

Question: Can different landmarks reuse the same approved name?

Answer & locked decision: Yes only at sufficient separation: no duplicates within 500 miles of each other. The same name may be reused beyond that distance.

Question 210 — Duplicate-name normalization

Question: Should capitalization and punctuation tricks bypass the 500-mile duplicate rule?

Answer & locked decision: No. Matching is case-insensitive and punctuation-insensitive, so cosmetic variants do not evade the restriction.

Question 211 — Potential naming conflicts

Question: Should software try to make every fuzzy duplicate-name judgment automatically?

Answer & locked decision: No. Show names that could possibly conflict to the approving admin. The system surfaces nearby/similar candidates; the admin makes the final judgment.

Question 212 — Naming approval context

Question: What should the admin see while approving a geographic name?

Answer & locked decision: Show the proposed name, feature type, proposing player, location/map context, and potentially conflicting nearby names. Also provide the ability to jump directly to the feature for inspection.

Question 213 — Admin jump

Question: May the admin jump-to-feature control use true teleportation even though player travel never does?

Answer & locked decision: Correct. Administrative teleportation is allowed for moderation/world management; ordinary character travel remains physical.

Question 214 — Invisible admin observation

Question: Should an admin inspecting a feature affect the world?

Answer & locked decision: No. Correct: observer mode is completely invisible and non-interactive. It creates no tracks, triggers no wildlife/NPC response, changes no discovery state, and leaves no world evidence.

Question 215 — Admin perception bypass

Question: Can observer mode ignore darkness, vegetation, walls, distance, rock, and similar perception barriers?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Admins may bypass normal visibility restrictions when needed for inspection.

Question 216 — Admin data overlays

Question: Should observer mode expose hidden world-system information?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Optional overlays can show elevation/terrain, feature boundaries, caves, resources, ownership, trails, and similar normally hidden data.

Question 217 — Permanent admin geography edits

Question: Can admins correct generated geography after world creation?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Allow permanent edits. Fixes such as malformed rivers, impossible terrain, or broken cave connections become persistent overrides to the procedural baseline.

Question 218 — Geography edit audit history

Question: Should permanent admin geography edits be auditable?

Answer & locked decision: Yes. Keep a simple history of who changed the geography, when, and a short reason. This provides accountability without requiring a full version-control system inside the game.
